Watering Mistakes Worth Avoiding

University extension guidance on the most common container watering errors, including how to tell overwatering apart from underwatering.

Small Space GardenOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Most container plant problems trace back to watering, and the counterintuitive part is that overwatering and underwatering produce surprisingly similar symptoms. Virginia Cooperative Extension's guidance on container watering lays out the mechanics clearly enough to tell the two apart.

The core principle: frequency, not volume

According to Virginia Cooperative Extension, "overwatering is not about how much water you apply; it's about how often you water." Each time you do water, the extension guidance recommends thoroughly soaking the potting soil until water runs out the drainage holes, rather than giving small, frequent sips that only wet the top layer. The mistake is watering too often, before the container actually needs it again, not watering too generously in any single session.

Why overwatered and underwatered plants can look alike

Both overwatered and underwatered plants commonly show wilting as a first symptom, according to Virginia Cooperative Extension, which is exactly why the two get confused. Beyond that shared symptom, the patterns diverge:

Overwatered plants typically show yellowing on both new and old leaves, softening or mushy leaves and stems, leaves falling off, stunted growth, rotten-smelling potting soil, and sometimes fungus gnats.

Underwatered plants typically show dry or brittle leaves, browning at the tips or edges, leaves curling (up or down), dry potting soil that has visibly shrunk away from the container's edges, and a container that feels unusually light when lifted.

That last underwatering sign, lifting the container to check its weight, is a genuinely useful quick check: a pot that feels surprisingly light likely has dry soil throughout, while a heavy pot that is still showing wilting is more likely dealing with waterlogged roots than lack of water.

Container size and material both change how fast soil dries

Virginia Cooperative Extension's guidance notes that taller containers drain more than shorter ones, and that container width does not meaningfully affect drainage the same way height does. Smaller containers also dry out faster than larger ones, because they simply hold less total water volume relative to how quickly a plant uses and loses water. This means a small pot and a large pot of the same plant type, side by side, genuinely need different watering frequencies, not the same schedule applied to both.

Material matters too: unglazed terracotta pots lose additional water through their porous walls, drying the rootzone faster than a nonporous plastic or glazed ceramic container would, all else being equal.

Drainage holes exist for a reason that is easy to undermine

The University of Maryland Extension's guidance on container vegetable gardening is specific about a commonly held but incorrect belief: adding rocks, gravel, or broken pot shards to the bottom of a container does not improve drainage. In fact, this practice can raise the effective water table inside the pot, leading to more waterlogging, not less. The correct approach is simply ensuring adequate drainage holes exist in the container itself, not adding a false "drainage layer" underneath the potting mix.

Saucers and trays need to be emptied

If a container sits in a saucer or tray to catch drained water, both Virginia Cooperative Extension and University of Maryland Extension note that this collected water should not be left sitting indefinitely. Virginia Cooperative Extension specifically recommends not leaving a container sitting in standing water for more than 60 minutes, since prolonged contact with standing water can produce the same waterlogging and root rot problems as overwatering directly. The University of Maryland Extension adds a second reason to empty saucers regularly: standing water can become a mosquito breeding site.

A simple pre-watering check

Rather than watering on a fixed calendar schedule, Virginia Cooperative Extension recommends checking the soil directly before each watering: stick a finger one to three inches into the potting mix (depth depending on container size) to feel for dryness, or lift the container to gauge its weight. Water only when the soil actually feels dry at that depth, not simply because a certain number of days have passed since the last watering.

Key takeaways

  • Overwatering is primarily about watering too often, not applying too much water in a single session; soak thoroughly, then wait until the soil is actually dry again.
  • Both overwatered and underwatered plants can wilt, but overwatering adds soft or yellowing leaves and rotten-smelling soil, while underwatering adds brittle, browning leaves and a container that feels unusually light.
  • Taller and smaller containers dry out faster than shorter or larger ones; unglazed terracotta dries faster than plastic or glazed ceramic.
  • Adding rocks or gravel to the bottom of a container does not improve drainage and can worsen waterlogging; adequate drainage holes in the container itself are what actually matters.
  • Empty saucers and drainage trays regularly; standing water for more than about an hour can cause root rot and also attract mosquitoes.

Checking the soil directly before watering, rather than following a fixed schedule, is the single habit extension guidance points to most consistently as the fix for both overwatering and underwatering.
