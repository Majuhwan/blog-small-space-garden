Seed Starting Indoors, a Timeline You Can Actually Follow

A university extension-backed guide to starting vegetable and flower seeds indoors, including why a windowsill is the wrong place to do it.

Small Space GardenOct 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Starting seeds indoors feels like it should be the easiest part of gardening: put a seed in dirt, add water, wait. University extension services, which exist specifically to translate research-based horticulture into practical home advice, point to several specific mistakes that quietly ruin indoor seed starting before a single seedling ever makes it outside.

Start with the "days until harvest" on the packet

Before planting anything, check the seed packet for its number of days until harvest, and compare that to your local last-frost date. University of Minnesota Extension's guidance is direct: this check is how you confirm a chosen variety will actually have time to ripen before frost returns in your area. Many long-season vegetables and many annual flowers specifically need an indoor head start if they're going to produce or bloom during the growing season at all; without that head start, direct-sowing them outdoors simply runs out of season.

Why a windowsill is the wrong location

This is the single most common seed-starting mistake extension guidance points to directly: a windowsill is not a good place to start seeds, even though it's the most obvious spot in most homes. The reasoning is specific. Windowsills tend to be the coldest part of a house at night and the hottest during the day, and most seeds need consistently warm soil to germinate well and build strong roots. Cooler nighttime soil temperatures on a windowsill can lead to seedling death from disease, while the daytime heat spike can dry out the potting mix fast enough to also kill seedlings. On top of the temperature swing, windowsill seedlings tend to grow too tall with thin, bent stems, because they're reaching for uneven light, and the setup risks water damage to the windowsill itself.

The better alternative, per the same guidance, is a dedicated spot away from heavy traffic, pets, cold drafts, and excess heat, with air temperatures kept above 60°F and consistent access to electrical outlets, because artificial grow lights are considered essential for healthy seedlings rather than optional. Natural light through a window gains strength through spring but is still relatively weak compared to a grow light positioned close to the plants, and cloudy spring days reduce it further.

Container choice matters more than it seems

Extension guidance recommends small, individual containers, specifically divided cell flats or small individual pots with a single seedling per container, rather than one large container with many seeds sown together. The reasoning is practical: seedlings grown together in one big container develop roots that tangle with their neighbors, and untangling that mess during transplanting damages the roots of all the seedlings involved. Every seed-starting container also needs drainage holes at the bottom, without exception.

If you're reusing plastic seed-starting containers from a previous season, extension guidance recommends sterilizing them first by soaking in a bleach or other disinfectant solution for 30 minutes, then rinsing, specifically to avoid carrying over diseases from whatever was grown in them before.

Seed storage, if you're not planting everything at once

Leftover seeds from a packet don't need to be thrown away. Extension guidance recommends not buying more seed than you'll use within two to three years, since germination rates decline the longer seed sits in storage, and storing unused seed packets in an airtight container in a cool place, ideally the refrigerator, to extend their viability. A small addition like a packet of silica gel, or a teaspoon of powdered milk wrapped in tissue, helps keep humidity low inside that storage container, which matters because moisture is what degrades stored seed fastest.

Hardening off before the move outdoors

Seedlings started indoors need a transition period before they go into the garden permanently, a process called hardening off. Extension guidance describes using a simple cold frame, adjusting its lid as needed to protect plants from freezing, and venting it a little further each day so seedlings gradually get used to outdoor wind and cold before facing it full-time. Once hardened off, seedlings should be transplanted on a cloudy day or in late afternoon, after the sun has passed its peak, since freshly transplanted seedlings often wilt when first exposed to full sun but typically recover within a day.

University of Maryland Extension's separate hardening-off guidance adds specific numbers worth following: begin the hardening process 1 to 2 weeks before moving plants outdoors, and don't put tender seedlings outside on windy days or when temperatures are below 45°F, since even cold-hardy plants can be damaged by freezing exposure before they've properly hardened. That same guidance also warns against overdoing it: hardening is meant to slow growth, not stop it, and carrying it too far can permanently stunt certain crops, with cauliflower and cucumbers specifically named as varieties that stop developing properly if hardened too aggressively.

A simple seed-starting checklist

  • Check the seed packet's days-to-harvest against your local last frost date before choosing what to start indoors.
  • Set up seedlings away from windowsills, in a spot with steady temperatures above 60°F and a grow light positioned close to the plants.
  • Use individual cell flats or small pots with drainage holes, one seedling per container, rather than one large shared container.
  • Sterilize reused containers in a diluted bleach solution for 30 minutes before reuse.
  • Store leftover seed in an airtight, cool container (the refrigerator works well) and avoid stockpiling more than two to three years' worth.
  • Harden off seedlings gradually before transplanting, and move them outdoors on a cloudy day or late afternoon.

Key takeaways

  • Match a seed variety's days-to-harvest against your last frost date before deciding what needs an indoor head start.
  • Windowsills cause temperature swings that commonly kill or weaken seedlings; a steady-temperature spot with a grow light performs better.
  • Individual cell containers with drainage holes beat one large shared container, because tangled roots get damaged during transplanting.
  • Store leftover seed in an airtight, cool container and don't buy more than you'll plant within two to three years.
  • Harden seedlings off gradually and transplant on a cloudy day or late afternoon to reduce transplant shock.

None of this requires special equipment beyond a grow light and some small containers. The timeline mistakes extension services flag most often are about location and container choice, not about anything exotic.
