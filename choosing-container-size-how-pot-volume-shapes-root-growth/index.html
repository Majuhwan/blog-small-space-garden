Choosing Container Size, How Pot Volume Shapes Root Growth

University extension container volume guidelines for common vegetables and herbs, and why a container that's too small limits growth more than light or water.

Small Space GardenOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

A plant that looks stunted despite plenty of sun and regular watering is often suffering from a problem that has nothing to do with light or water: the container itself is too small. University of Maryland Extension's guidance on container vegetables lays out specific minimum volumes by plant type, which turns "is this pot big enough" from a guess into a lookup.

Why container size limits growth independent of care

Extension guidance is direct about the principle: match container size to plant size, considering both the top growth and the root system, and don't squeeze large plants into small containers, because restricting root growth too much prevents the plant from growing well regardless of how well you otherwise care for it. Depth and total volume both matter, and extension notes that a wider, shallower container holding the same total volume as a taller, narrower one will be more stable in wind, an easily overlooked detail for anything grown on an exposed balcony or rooftop.

Minimum container volumes by plant

Pulled directly from University of Maryland Extension's published guidance:

Vegetable Minimum container volume
Radishes 1 pint
Beets 1/2 gallon
Lettuce (leaf) 1/2 gallon
Beans (bush) 2 gallon
Carrots 2 gallon
Swiss chard 2 gallon
Tomatoes (cherry) 2 gallon
Cabbage 5 gallon
Cucumbers 5 gallon
Eggplant 5 gallon
Peppers 5 gallon
Tomatoes (standard) 5 gallon
Squash (summer) 10 gallon

For herbs specifically, the same extension guidance splits them by growth habit rather than giving a single number: large-statured herbs (rosemary, bay leaf, sage, parsley, lavender, dill, and fennel) need a 5-gallon or larger pot, while small-statured herbs (basil, cilantro, thyme, mint, oregano, tarragon, and marjoram) grow well in 2- to 5-gallon containers.

The gap between categories is the useful part of this table. A radish needs barely a pint of soil, while a standard tomato plant needs 10 times that volume just to meet the minimum, and a summer squash needs 20 times as much. Treating all vegetables as interchangeable in a uniform set of pots is one of the most common reasons a balcony garden looks great in May and struggles by July: the fast-growing, high-volume plants simply run out of room first.

Bush versus full-size varieties change the math

Extension guidance notes that vegetable varieties specifically bred or labeled as bush, dwarf, or compact can be grown in smaller containers than their full-size counterparts in the same table. A "patio" or "bush" tomato variety, for example, is bred to stay more compact above ground and generally needs less root volume than a standard indeterminate tomato, even though both appear in the same "tomatoes" row of a generic size chart. Checking the variety name on the seed packet or plant tag against this distinction before assuming the 5-gallon minimum applies is worth the extra minute.

Why "air pruning" changes the calculation for fabric pots

University of Maryland Extension also addresses a container type that doesn't follow the same volume math as rigid plastic or ceramic pots: fabric grow bags. These are made from porous natural or synthetic material, and extension explains the mechanism directly: when roots reach the open air at the edge of a fabric bag, they stop growing in length and instead branch into a denser, more fibrous root system, rather than hitting a solid wall and continuing to grow in one direction as they would in a non-porous pot. This "air pruning" effect means a fabric container can support healthy growth at a given volume that might otherwise feel tight in solid plastic, because the root system itself grows differently inside it.

Depth matters separately from total volume

A container can hit its target gallon volume while still being too shallow for a particular plant's root structure. Carrots, for instance, need enough vertical depth for the root itself to extend without curling or forking, which a wide, shallow 2-gallon tray won't necessarily provide even if the total volume matches the chart. When choosing a container shape, check whether the plant's root system is naturally deep (carrots, standard tomatoes) or shallow and spreading (lettuce, most herbs), and let that guide the proportions, not just the total gallon count.

Key takeaways

  • Minimum container volume varies enormously by plant: radishes need about a pint, while summer squash needs roughly 10 gallons, according to University of Maryland Extension's published guidance.
  • Restricting root volume limits plant growth regardless of light or watering quality, which is why an undersized pot can look like a care problem when it's actually a sizing problem.
  • Bush, dwarf, or compact varieties can be grown in smaller containers than the full-size version of the same vegetable.
  • Fabric grow bags cause roots to branch through air pruning rather than circling, which can make a given volume perform better than the same volume in a solid plastic pot.
